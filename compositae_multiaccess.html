Help with botanical words

A fruit tuft is attached to each fruit. Scales or hairs on the flower platform are attached to the platform. They are different structures.

Flower head (capitulum)
The whole daisy-like or thistle-like head. It is made of many tiny individual flowers, called florets.
Outer strap (ligule or ray)
A flat, petal-like part of a tiny flower. A dandelion has straps throughout its head; a daisy has them only around the outside.
Head bracts (phyllaries)
The small leaf-like or papery pieces around the outside and base of a flower head.
Bract cup (involucre)
All the head bracts taken together. Measure across or along this cup only, not including the outer flower straps.
Flower platform (receptacle)
The solid base inside the head where the little flowers attach. Open a head carefully lengthwise or remove some flowers to see it.
Scales between flowers (paleae)
Small bracts attached to the flower platform between individual flowers. They stay on the platform when a fruit is removed.
Fruit (achene)
The little dry, one-seeded fruit often called a seed. Use ripe fruits where the key asks about shape, ribs, beaks or hairs.
Fruit tuft (pappus)
Hairs, bristles, scales or a tiny crown attached to the TOP OF EACH FRUIT. These are different from hairs or scales attached to the flower platform.
Feathery (plumose)
A hair or bristle with many fine side branches, like a feather. Check with a lens.
Beak
A narrow extension at the top of the fruit, below its tuft.
Attachment scar (hilum)
The little mark near the base or side of a fruit where it was attached to the flower platform.
Style
The slender part rising from the top of the fruit inside a flower; its tip often forks into two branches.
Pollen sacs (anthers)
The pollen-bearing parts of a flower. Their stalks are called filaments. Tiny tails at the bases of pollen sacs need close magnification.
Bisexual flower
One tiny flower with both male and female parts. Female flowers lack working male parts; sterile flowers do not produce fruits.
Opposite / alternate leaves
Opposite leaves come in pairs at the same point on a stem. Alternate leaves are attached singly, at different heights.
Rosette / basal leaves
Leaves crowded near ground level. Stem leaves grow higher up the stem.
Divided once, twice or three times
A divided leaf has deep cuts toward its middle vein. If those divisions are cut again, it is divided twice; a further round makes three times.
Wing
A flat strip along a stem or fruit, not an insect wing.
Annual / biennial / perennial
An annual completes its life in one growing season; a biennial usually takes two; a perennial lives for several years.
Runner / underground stem
A runner creeps along the ground and roots at joints. An underground stem (rhizome) grows below the soil.
Gland / glandular
A small structure producing sticky liquid or other substances. Some glands are on the tips of hairs.
Papery / felted / cobwebby
Papery parts are thin and dry. Felted hairs make a dense soft covering. Cobwebby hairs form a loose, fine web.
Cross-section
The shape seen looking at a cut end. A fruit can be round, angular or flattened across its width.
mm / cm
10 millimetres (mm) = 1 centimetre (cm). Use a small ruler and a hand lens.

    What this preliminary key can tell you

    All 174 original question pairs are available. The source has 175 routes to 132 distinct result labels, representing 129 genera. The extra labels name Cirsium section Epitrachys and two particular Centaurea species. Those labels stay visible within their genus routes.

    • A missing entry is unknown, not “absent”. A selected answer removes a route only where that route records the opposite choice. Routes that never asked the question remain possible.
    • Separate routes stay separate. A genus remains if at least one of its complete routes is compatible. Answers from different routes are never joined to manufacture a match.
    • Some features are combinations. The original choices, measurements and exceptions are kept together. Selecting one means the complete description fits; matching just one phrase is not enough.
    • Some distinctions apply only in a group. The group label provides context, not a new observed character. Choosing a feature in one group does not imply that your plant belongs to that group.
    • Many candidates may remain. A route-based key has gaps and does not describe every character for every genus. “Not recorded” results are possibilities, not positive matches. The suggestions favour questions that can reduce the present list; they are not confidence scores.
    • Confirm the result. Names and spelling follow the existing historical key. No modern name review, species-level verification or independent checking against Davis was performed for this adaptation.

    Use ripe fruits, a small ruler and a hand lens when required. Some details need a microscope. If an organ is missing or too small to see, skip it.
